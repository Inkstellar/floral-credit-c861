01 / Overview

The short version

A school can run on registers for years. The trouble starts when the same child appears in six of them and none of the six agree.

I built a school management app around one idea: model the school once, then give every role its own view of that same model. Admissions, students, attendance, fees, exams, library, transport and communication share one academic structure instead of a spreadsheet per department.

The result is a full-stack TypeScript application with an admin workspace, teacher and student portals, a parent portal, and a public school website — one codebase, one data model, four different front doors.

This page is the story of the decisions behind it: what I got right early, what I got wrong, and what the app looks like now.

02 / Starting Point

Where I started

The first version worked. That was the problem.

The app began as a skeleton: students, teachers, admissions, fees and attendance, each with basic CRUD screens and a dashboard for each role. Every screen loaded, every table saved, and a demo looked convincing.

Then the seams started to show. A class was free text in one form and a number in another. A fee belonged to a student but not to an academic year. An attendance record knew a date but not which timetable slot it belonged to. The public website and the ERP lived in the same repository and shared almost nothing.

I had built screens. What I had not built was a model of a school.
03 / The Problem

What the problem turned out to be

The missing feature was never the problem. The problem was that every module had its own idea of the school.

When a parent asked for a receipt, the office searched a fee sheet. When a teacher asked who was absent, the office searched an attendance register. When the principal asked how the term was going, someone copied numbers from both into a third sheet. Each answer was plausible, and none of them could be trusted together.

  • Academic structure was implicit — classes, sections and years were typed into forms rather than chosen from one shared structure.
  • Money was a status, not a ledger — fees were marked paid or unpaid with no structure, fine, discount, receipt or accounting entry behind them.
  • Every role needed a different slice of the same data, but each dashboard queried its own slice in its own way.
  • The public site and the ERP were two products sharing a repository, with no shared navigation or vocabulary.
04 / Decision One

Model the academic year before building screens

Before another screen went in, I made the academic structure explicit: academic year → class group → class → section, with class–section mapping, subjects and schedules hanging off it. Students, attendance, timetables, exams and fees all point at that structure.

That changed the shape of every later feature. Attendance is taken against a class–section on a date. A fee structure belongs to a year and a class. A report card is a student’s results in an exam within a year. When the school rolls over, the structure changes and the history stays intact.

The cost was weeks of work with nothing visible to show. The return was that “which year?” stopped being a question the rest of the app had to ask.
05 / Decision Two

One permission model, four shells

Admins, teachers, parents and students need different things from the same records — not four copies of the app.

I kept one data model and one permission matrix, then gave each role its own shell. Admins get the module rail and a workspace built around the whole school. Teachers get the classes they teach, attendance and marks. Parents get their children, fees, results and PTM bookings. Students get their own attendance, homework, fees and transport.

Route guards use the same roles the permission matrix does, so a URL a role cannot use fails closed. The public website stays deliberately outside this shell: a parent applying for admission should never meet a login wall.

Admin

The whole school, module by module.

Teacher

Today’s classes, attendance and marks.

Parent

Every child, fee and result in one place.

Student

Their own timetable, homework, attendance and transport.

06 / Decision Three

Dashboards as programs, not bespoke pages

Every role opens on a dashboard, and every dashboard was becoming a hand-built page of cards, charts and tables. Each one was slightly different, and each change had to be made in several places.

I moved the layout into OpenUI. Each dashboard is a small OpenUI Lang program rendered against a component library built from the app’s own cards and charts. The revenue dashboard is the clearest example: the backend returns one summary payload — collected, outstanding, spend, monthly trend, payment mix, recent receipts — and a builder turns it into a program. The renderer draws the page.

The same program could later be written by a model without changing the components or the styling. Two things came out of it: dashboards became consistent without a design review for each one, and the page became data, which is easier to test and easier to change.
07 / What Shipped

What shipped

The app is now a set of connected journeys rather than a menu of modules.

The admin workspace covers the school year end to end. The three portals give each role the part of that year they actually use.

  • Admissions to enrolment — public application, enquiry, decision, then a student record in the right year, class and section.
  • The school day — timetable, daily attendance for students and staff, teacher marks, assignments and announcements.
  • Money — fee structures, fines and discounts, reminders, online payments, receipts, accounting and a revenue view.
  • The wider operation — library circulation, transport routes and live map, visitors, medical room, HR and payroll, ID cards and certificates.
  • The record — roles, permissions, custom fields, audit log and exports.
08 / UX Process

Design process artifacts

Before any screen was built, the structure of the school had to be understood. These artifacts guided the information architecture, permission model, and user flows.

09 / Outcome

A full school year, in one system

The app now covers the year from an enquiry at the front desk to a report card at the end of term.

The office can answer a fee question without leaving the screen. A teacher can take attendance against the right class and section, and the same record feeds the parent’s view. The principal’s overview is built from the same data the accounts are.

None of this replaces the school’s judgement. A timetable still has to be built, a fee dispute still has to be settled by a person, and a parent still wants to speak to a teacher. What the app removes is the copying between registers, the “which sheet is current?” question, and the queue at the office for a receipt.

10 / Looking back

Looking back

If I did it again, I would still model the academic year first. That decision did more for the app than any individual screen.

What I would change is the order of the rest. I would build the parent portal earlier, because parents ask the questions that expose whether the data model is actually coherent. I would add a self-test mode and a debug bridge before the module count passed twenty, not after. And I would move the dashboards to OpenUI sooner: it arrived late, and it should have been the default from the first admin screen.

The next version is about depth rather than breadth: fewer modules with better empty states, clearer errors, and a mobile-first attendance flow for teachers walking between classrooms. A school does not need more software. It needs less paperwork.

Good software starts with a school that will use it.

The best way to understand the app is to follow a school day: an application at the front desk, a register in a classroom, a receipt at the office, a result at home.
